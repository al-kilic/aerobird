15 January 2009. US Airways 1549 departs LaGuardia and strikes a flock of Canada geese in the climb. The crew ditches in the Hudson.

Wildlife strikes are a low-altitude problem, concentrated in take-off, climb-out, approach and landing. That is the band AeroBird forecasts.

Altitude at impact per the NTSB accident report

Why public bird data misleads

Most bird maps count birdwatchers.

No effort correction

A reservoir with ten birders logs more birds than a field with one, whatever the birds are doing. Unless counts are divided by survey effort, the map shows where people look.

No records airside

The inner ring sits behind the airport fence, so public records thin out toward the runway. That is where aircraft are lowest and the data is weakest.

Nights go uncounted

Much songbird migration happens at night and at altitude. Daytime checklists miss it. Weather radar records it.

Method

Four layers. One answer per ring.

  1. L4

    Airside logs Your wildlife team

    Patrol and dispersal logs from your wildlife team. They are the only source inside the fence. The data stays yours.

  2. L3

    Species & season Effort-corrected

    eBird records divided by survey effort. Complete checklists supply true zeros. The result is a week-by-week climatology.

  3. L2

    Weather radar Altitude, day & night

    European weather-radar bird profiles give density by altitude, day and night. They cover the hours and heights checklists don’t.

  4. L1

    Strike history Calibration and testing

    Reported strikes with species, phase of flight, height and damage. Severity is fitted to them. Forecasts are tested against them.

The model

Risk=κΣ Hazard×Severity×Exposure

Hazard

Birds per survey-hour, per species

Bird abundance per species, corrected for survey effort. Flocking species are scored on their bad days as well as the mean.

Severity

Damage given a strike

Scales with body mass and is fitted to recorded strike outcomes. A 3 kg goose and a 20 g swallow are different events.

Exposure

Time spent low in each ring

On a 3° glidepath an aircraft is at about 1,600 ft at 5 NM and 3,200 ft at 10 NM. Departures cross the same rings at different heights and speeds. That is why arrival and departure risk differ.

Expected damaging strikes per 100,000 movements Each score carries an uncertainty interval and a data grade. κ is calibrated on reported strike history. No score goes live until the model beats a movements-only baseline on held-out airports.

Many birds migrate after dark.

Observers go home at dusk. Radar keeps recording. Switch the demo to night to see the altitude profile and the score change.

    House rules

    Rule 1

    No data means no score.

    A ring without enough evidence shows no score. It never defaults to low. Step to early January in the demo to see it.

    Rule 2

    Every number has an interval.

    Each score shows its uncertainty interval and a data grade. Thin evidence gets a wide interval and a lower grade.

    Rule 3

    Beat the baseline or stay dark.

    Before any score goes live, the model must beat a movements-only baseline on held-out airports. If it doesn’t, nothing is published.
